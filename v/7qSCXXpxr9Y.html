TEKKEN - The Motion Picture (1997) Full Movie

films · cartoon // Aired 2026-08-08

Summary

A 1997 Japanese animated film adapting Namco's Tekken, the 3D fighting game that had arrived in arcades a few years earlier and become one of the defining series of the PlayStation era. Released direct to video in Japan and distributed internationally on VHS, it runs about an hour and is animated in the style its moment demanded: hard shadows, heavy cel work, and fight choreography that borrows its poses directly from the game's move lists.\n\nThe story pulls from the games' central thread — the Mishima family, a father and son locked in a mutual attempt at destruction, and a martial arts tournament that exists mostly to bring the cast into the same building. Kazuya Mishima carries the film; Heihachi is the reason there is anything to carry.\n\nIt is worth watching with the right expectations. This is not an adaptation that tries to justify itself to people who have never played the game — it assumes you know who these people are and gets on with putting them in rooms together.

Why it matters

Video game adaptations in the nineties were a genre defined almost entirely by failure, and this sits in the middle of that story. The live-action Street Fighter and Mortal Kombat films had just proved the audience existed and that the results would rarely be good; the animated route, taken here and by Street Fighter II: The Animated Movie a few years earlier, was the industry's other attempt at the problem.\n\nAnimation solved something live action could not. A fighting game is a series of impossible bodies doing impossible things, and drawing them is cheaper and more faithful than staging them. The films from this period that still hold up are mostly the animated ones, for exactly that reason.\n\nIt is also a document of how Japanese studios treated their own game properties before the current era of prestige adaptations. This was made as merchandise — something to sell alongside the game, on tape, to people who already owned the game. That the result has any craft in it at all is the interesting part, and it is why the direct-to-video anime of the nineties is now collected and argued over rather than forgotten.
