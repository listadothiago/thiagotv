Summary

An 84-minute print of The Time Machine, adapted from the H. G. Wells novel, in a copy the uploader describes as having been found at a yard sale. Wells's 1895 novella — a Victorian inventor travelling forward to find humanity split into the passive Eloi and the subterranean Morlocks — has been filmed several times, and prints of these adaptations circulate widely in varying quality.\n\nThe running time and the provenance are worth noting rather than glossing over: this is a collector's copy rather than a restoration, and the version differs from the standard release length.

Why it matters

Wells's novella is one of the foundational texts of science fiction. It gave the language the term 'time machine' and, more importantly, established time travel as a device for social argument rather than adventure: the future it describes is the British class system carried to its biological conclusion, with the leisured and the labouring evolved into separate species.\n\nAdaptations have tended to keep the machine and soften the argument, which is itself a useful thing to notice about how science fiction moves between print and film. The visual conception of the machine — brass, velvet, a spinning disc — comes from screen adaptation rather than from Wells, and has become more familiar than anything in the book.\n\nIts influence runs through effectively all subsequent time-travel fiction, and the Eloi and Morlocks remain a standard shorthand for a society divided into the ornamental and the exploited.

Controversy

Prints of older films circulating outside official channels vary enormously in completeness and quality, and running times are often the giveaway. A viewer should treat an unlabelled 84-minute version as potentially cut, re-edited or sourced from a television print rather than as the film as released — which is part of the character of watching this way, but worth knowing.
