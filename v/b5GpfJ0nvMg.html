Summary

The second episode of Jem, the animated series produced from 1985 by Sunbow Productions and Marvel Productions for the toy company Hasbro, and developed by Christy Marx.\n\nThe premise is a double life. Jerrica Benton inherits her father's record company, Starlight Music, along with the foster home it supports and a hidden holographic computer called Synergy. Synergy projects a complete disguise, letting Jerrica appear as the rock star Jem, front the band the Holograms, and fight for the company against a hostile business partner — while the rival band the Misfits sabotage her at every opportunity.\n\nWhat separates Jem from its contemporaries is the music. Nearly every episode stops for two or three original songs, staged as fully realised music videos with their own editing, effects and costume changes rather than as background score. Hundreds of songs were written across the run, an enormous commitment for a syndicated cartoon, and the format only makes sense as a response to what MTV had done to how young audiences consumed music.\n\nVisually the show is pure mid-eighties excess — asymmetric hair, layered neon, shoulder pads — and it commits to that completely, which is a large part of why it has aged into something people watch deliberately rather than nostalgically.

Why it matters

Jem belongs to a very specific moment in American television. Deregulation of children's broadcasting in the early 1980s removed the barriers that had kept programmes from functioning as extended advertisements, and a wave of series built around toy lines followed. Sunbow and Marvel made Transformers, G.I. Joe and My Little Pony out of the same building; Jem was Hasbro's attempt at the same trick aimed at girls.\n\nThat origin is usually treated as damning, and it is worth being precise about why Jem escaped it. The show had a female creator, a largely female cast of characters with distinct motivations, and storylines about running a business, keeping a foster home solvent and competing professionally — unusually concrete ambitions for a girls' cartoon of the period, where the alternative was largely ponies and princesses. The Misfits, in particular, were allowed to be genuinely unpleasant rather than softened, which gave the series stakes.\n\nIts musical format also turned out to be more durable than the toy line, which was discontinued relatively quickly. The songs circulate independently of the show, and the series' visual language — the split identity, the holographic transformation, the band-versus-band structure — recurs constantly in later animation and pop music imagery, from Japanese idol series to virtual bands.\n\nThe show has since become a reference point in discussions of eighties animation aimed at girls, and its reputation has risen substantially. It is now read less as a toy advertisement than as one of the few places that decade's television let young women be ambitious, competitive and loud.

Controversy

The commercial logic behind the entire genre remains contested. Series like this were made to sell toys, and critics at the time argued — correctly, as a matter of fact — that deregulation had produced programme-length advertisements aimed at children too young to recognise them as such. That criticism applies to Jem as much as to its stable-mates, whatever its other merits.\n\nThe 2015 live-action film adaptation is the sharper controversy. Directed by Jon M. Chu, it discarded almost everything distinctive about the premise — Synergy, the science fiction element, the scale of the band's world — in favour of a small-scale story about internet fame. Fans of the series objected loudly and publicly during production, the finished film was poorly received, and it was withdrawn from cinemas after roughly two weeks, an unusually fast retreat for a wide release. It is now a standard example in arguments about adapting cult properties while removing the things the cult actually cared about.
