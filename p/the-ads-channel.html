From the station

Two archives of television nobody meant to keep

2026-08-08

Every channel on this station is a genre except one. The Ads channel is a filing cabinet.

It holds 129 programmes, and they come from two entirely separate acts of hoarding. Neither was made to be kept. Commercials are the most disposable thing television produces — written to run for a season, priced to be forgotten, wiped or discarded once the campaign ended. What survives survives by accident, because somebody left a tape running and then, decades later, put it online.

The Grupo Imagem tapes

The first hoard is Brazilian, and it is oddly specific: a run of teleshopping spots carrying the name Grupo Imagem Teleshop, dated between 1990 and 2000, most of them from the middle nineties. Several carry a São Paulo phone number in the title — the 011 area code, back when you dialled a number on screen rather than tapping a link.

The products are the whole argument. Grupo Imagem Teleshop: AURI - 1995, Grupo Imagem Teleshop: Micro-Crisp - 1995, Grupo Imagem Teleshop: Dental White - 1995, Grupo Imagem Teleshop: Knit Wit - 1997 — a cosmetic, a kitchen gadget, a tooth whitener, a knitting device. Add the knife set that turns up in Grupo Imagem Teleshop: Facas Ginsu 2000 - 1994 and you have the entire genre in five objects: things that solve a problem you did not know you had, demonstrated by hands you never see the owner of, sold at a price repeated more often than the product name.

Teleshopping is worth taking seriously as a form. It is the only television that admits what all television is doing. There is no programme wrapped around it, no pretence of a story: it is a person talking directly at you for several uninterrupted minutes, with a phone number burned into the corner the entire time. Everything that later became normal online — the demonstration video, the countdown, the bundled offer, the testimonial from someone who is definitely not an actor — was worked out here first, on tape, in a studio with a curtain behind it.

The Brazilian context matters too. This is the decade after the Plano Real, when inflation stopped eating money between the shop and the till and consumer credit reached households that had never had it. Selling a hundred-real gadget in twelve instalments over the phone only makes sense in an economy where twelve months from now is a meaningful concept. These tapes are a record of that shift as much as of the products.

The reels that were here

For a while this channel also held something bigger and blunter: a series of compilation tapes of American commercials from the 1980s, numbered past a hundred. They were not curated, which was their whole value — a curated reel gives you the famous ads, the ones that won prizes and turn up in documentaries. These gave you whatever happened to be on the tape: the local dealership next to the national soft drink, the public service announcement, the station ident, the trailer for a film nobody remembers. Less a highlight reel than a core sample.

They have since been retired from the station, because they were an hour long each and this channel works better as short things. Something shorter will take their place. The observation they supported is worth keeping anyway: what comes through in that kind of footage is texture rather than content — the grain of tape copied a few times, the way an eighties ad is mixed louder than the programme around it, the fact that each one is trying to produce a slightly different feeling in under thirty seconds, and that watching two hundred in a row makes all of those feelings cancel out into something closer to weather.

Two outliers

Two things in the channel are not archive material at all.

How to Make Godzilla Really Angry is a cinema PSA made by the Alamo Drafthouse — an advertisement that is also a joke about advertising, and one of the rare examples of a venue building an identity out of telling its own customers off. It sits in this channel because it is technically a commercial, and it makes an argument the other 127 cannot: that the form can be used deliberately and well.

Freak Phone Commercial (120 Minutes late 80's) is a late-eighties phone-line commercial, the kind that ran in the small hours around music programming. It is worth watching next to the teleshopping spots, because it is doing exactly the same thing with none of the respectability.

Why keep them on a television

The rest of this station is programmes: films, cartoons, documentaries, music. Advertising was the thing that paid for all of it, and it was the thing everyone in the room agreed to ignore. Putting it on its own channel inverts that — here the ad is not the interruption, it is the broadcast.

Turn the dial to Ads and you get something no streaming service will ever offer you, because there is no rights holder motivated to offer it: television exactly as boring and specific and commercial as it actually was.
